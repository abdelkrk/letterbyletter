Robert opens an envelope. Inside is a small slip of paper telling him exactly how much money will arrive, every month, for the rest of his life. Across town, Maya looks at a screen. It shows her a single number: her account balance. It went up last month. It might go down next month.

Both of these are retirement. But they are not the same thing. One is a pension. The other is a 401(k). And the difference between them isn't just paperwork. It's a difference in who promises what, who bears the risk, and who makes the decisions.

Let's start with the pension, because it's the older idea. Picture a factory, decades ago. A young man named Robert takes a job there. He clocks in. He works. And from his very first year, he's enrolled in the company's pension plan. Every year he stays adds to his years of service. Every year, his salary is recorded. And a formula, set by the plan, combines those two numbers, years of service and salary history, into a promise: a defined benefit, calculated in advance, that the company is obligated to pay him once he retires.

Notice what Robert does not have. He doesn't have a personal account with his name on it. What he has is a claim, a promise, backed by the plan. Behind that promise sits a pooled pension fund. His employer contributes to it. So do the contributions tied to every other worker at the plant. It isn't Robert's money sitting in a separate box. It's one large pool, managed professionally, invested over time, meant to cover the promised benefits of everyone in the plan, for as long as they live.

That distinction matters when markets move. When the fund's investments rise, the pool grows. When they fall, the pool shrinks, and a funding gap can appear. But here's the key part: Robert's promised benefit doesn't shrink with it. The fund moves. The promise stays defined by the plan. It's the employer, guided by actuarial assumptions about how long retirees are expected to live, who is responsible for keeping the fund adequately financed, adding more if a gap opens up. The fund absorbs the ups and downs. The worker, in principle, does not.

There's another kind of risk a pension plan is built to absorb: longevity. Some retirees live five years past retirement. Others live thirty. In a pension plan, that risk is pooled across the whole group, so no individual worker has to guess how long their own money needs to last. Where a plan promises it, the result can be a lifetime monthly income, for as long as the retiree lives. Private pensions like this also sit inside a federal insurance framework, within limits, meant to offer a backstop if a plan fails, though that protection has its own boundaries.

So to summarize the pension structure: the plan manages pooled assets, promises a formula-based benefit, and bears most of the funding and investment responsibility. The worker earns a defined promise, not a personal balance.

Now let's look at what happened to that structure, starting with a law most people have never read: the Employee Retirement Income Security Act, ERISA, signed in 1974. ERISA didn't invent pensions. It regulated them. It set federal standards for participation, vesting, and disclosure. It imposed fiduciary duties and funding rules on the people managing these plans. And it created the framework behind that pension insurance system for many private defined benefit plans, within limits. What ERISA did not do is require every employer to offer a pension in the first place.

Four years later, in 1978, Congress passed the Revenue Act of 1978, and buried inside the tax code was a new section: Section 401(k). This is important to get right, because it's often misunderstood: Section 401(k) was a new legal option. It allowed a new kind of salary-deferral arrangement. It was not an immediate switch, and it did not replace pensions overnight. In the early 1980s, the IRS clarified how these arrangements could work, and employers slowly began to adopt them. At first, many companies offered a 401(k) alongside their existing pension plan, not instead of it. Through the 1980s, then the 1990s, then the 2000s, the balance gradually shifted. Today, defined contribution plans like the 401(k) are far more prevalent in private industry than they once were, but defined benefit pensions still exist. This was a gradual structural change, unfolding over decades. Not one law, and not one date.

So let's walk through what a 401(k) actually looks like, using Maya as our example. Maya takes a job, and her employer offers a 401(k) plan. She elects a contribution percentage, and going forward, that percentage of her paycheck is automatically directed into her own individual account. Where the plan offers one, her employer may add a matching contribution on top. This money doesn't go into one shared fund with everyone else. It goes into an account that belongs to Maya alone.

She's then given a menu of investment options, and she chooses where to put her money. As her contributions accumulate and the market moves, her account balance rises, unevenly, the way real markets actually behave, not a smooth, guaranteed line. When the market dips, her balance drops too, directly and immediately. There's no employer standing between her and that decline, absorbing it on her behalf. When the market recovers, her balance can recover as well, though recovery is possible, never guaranteed.

The math behind her final balance is simple to state, harder to control: contributions, plus investment returns, minus fees, minus withdrawals. Fees quietly reduce what accumulates over the years, which is why they matter even when they seem small. And notice what her employer has and hasn't promised. The employer promises contributions, not a final benefit amount. Nobody can tell Maya today exactly what her account will be worth when she retires. Some plans simplify these choices with features like automatic enrollment or target-date funds, but the core structure stays the same: it's her account, her elections, her balance.

Put Robert and Maya side by side, and ten structural differences come into focus.

The pension promises a formula-based benefit. The 401(k) defines contributions, not a guaranteed final benefit. The pension pools its assets across every participant. The 401(k) is one individual account. Investment risk sits mainly with the plan sponsor in a pension; in a 401(k), it lands directly on the participant. A pension's growth follows salary and service, largely outside the worker's daily control. A 401(k)'s outcome depends heavily on the worker's own saving decisions. In a pension, professionals manage the pooled fund. In a 401(k), the participant chooses among a menu of options themselves. Pension benefits are often tied to the original plan and years of service at that employer. 401(k) balances, once vested, generally move with the worker from job to job. A pension typically pays recurring income for life. A 401(k) produces a balance that the participant must convert into income themselves. A pension pools longevity risk across a large group. A 401(k) participant bears substantially more responsibility for making sure their own money lasts. And a pension creates a long-term, promised-benefit liability for the employer, while a 401(k) creates only a defined contribution obligation, with no guarantee attached to the final outcome.

Underneath all of it is one more shift, maybe the most important one: responsibilities that used to sit with the institution, saving enough, investing wisely, monitoring the account, moving it when jobs change, planning how to draw it down in retirement, have moved to sit with the individual worker instead.

That's the real thesis here. The system didn't just change the account. It changed who decides, and who bears the risk.

Why did this happen? Not for one single reason. Employers weighed the regulatory and accounting complexity of long-term pension obligations against the more predictable, defined-contribution-style costs of a 401(k) plan. At the same time, workers increasingly valued portability, the ability to carry retirement savings with them as they changed jobs, industries, and careers, across a labor market that was itself becoming less centered on long careers at a single employer. None of this makes either side a villain. Employers managing funding risk and workers wanting mobility are both acting reasonably within the incentives they face. Multiple factors contributed. There was no single cause.

And this shift did bring real gains. A 401(k) account is portable: Maya can carry her vested balance with her when she changes jobs, and it stays hers. It's visible: she can see her balance whenever she wants, rather than waiting on a distant formula. She has more control over her own investment choices, within the plan's menu. And where her employer offers a match, that's additional money that simply wasn't part of the pension structure in the same way.

But gains came bundled with new responsibilities: choosing a contribution rate, choosing an investment allocation, absorbing market volatility, paying attention to fees, deciding what to do with the balance during a rollover, and planning withdrawals carefully enough that the money lasts. Neither system is simply free money. They allocate responsibility differently.

So where does this leave things today? It's not one uniform picture. Some private-sector workers have a 401(k). Some private-sector workers still have a pension. Some have both. Public-sector workers often participate in a separate pension system of their own. Some workers have no employer-sponsored plan at all, and self-employed workers often rely on other retirement arrangements entirely. And underneath every one of these, there's a separate federal layer that exists regardless of what an employer offers: Social Security.

Strip away the specifics, and the two structures reduce to this. On one side: an employer or plan manages pooled assets, promises a formula-based benefit, and bears most of the funding and investment responsibility. On the other side: worker and employer contributions build an individual account, its value rises and falls with the market, and the final resources available in retirement equal whatever that balance turns out to be.

Over the past half-century, American retirement didn't flip from one of these to the other overnight. It moved, gradually, from promised income managed by an institution, toward an individual account managed substantially by the worker. More risk, more decisions, and more portability shifted onto the individual. But employer responsibility didn't vanish completely, plan design, administration, and fiduciary duties are still part of the picture. What changed, structurally, is where the weight of that responsibility now sits.
