CORE HISTORICAL TIMELINE
======================================================================

1. "The Baby Killer" report
   Claim: War on Want (British charity) published an investigation by journalist
   Mike Muller in 1974, titled "The Baby Killer," accusing formula marketing of
   contributing to infant illness/death in poor communities.
   Source: War on Want, "The Baby Killer" (1974); summarized in Wikibooks
   "Professionalism/The Nestlé Infant Formula Scandal"; NBER working paper w24452.
   Certainty: FACT
   Used in AR: yes (beat 039) | Used in EN: yes (beat 039)

2. German/Swiss pamphlet retitling and libel case
   Claim: A Swiss-published version of the pamphlet was retitled "Nestlé tötet
   Babys" ("Nestlé Kills Babies"). Nestlé sued for libel; a Swiss court found the
   literal title libelous but the judge warned the company that if it did not
   want to be accused of causing death/illness through its sales practices
   (e.g. sales reps dressed as nurses), it should change how it did business.
   Source: Reporting on the 1976 Swiss verdict, summarized in Wikibooks
   "Professionalism/The Nestlé Infant Formula Scandal."
   Certainty: FACT (title/suit/verdict); PRIMARY-SOURCE ALLEGATION (exact wording
   of judge's remarks, widely paraphrased across secondary sources)
   Used in AR: yes (beats 041, 043-044) | Used in EN: yes (beats 041, 043-044)

3. "Milk nurses" — sales representatives dressed as medical staff
   Claim: Formula companies employed saleswomen who dressed and behaved like
   nurses, distributing free samples and promoting formula inside hospitals and
   maternity wards, lending the product implied medical authority.
   Source: Multiple secondary histories (Wikibooks; Ranker "Remembering the
   Nestlé Baby Formula Scandal"; AAP Pediatrics 1981 summary of WHO Code
   background); consistent across independent accounts.
   Certainty: DOCUMENTED MARKETING PRACTICE
   Used in AR: yes (beats 007-008, 018) | Used in EN: yes (beats 007-008, 018)

4. INFACT boycott, 1977
   Claim: A U.S. consumer group, Infant Formula Action Coalition (INFACT),
   launched a boycott of the company's products in 1977 (Minneapolis origin),
   which spread nationally then internationally (Canada, UK, Australia, etc.)
   and became one of the longest-running consumer boycotts against a single
   multinational, continuing into the 1980s.
   Source: Commons Library / Global Nonviolent Action Database entry "International
   groups boycott Nestle products... 1977-1984."
   Certainty: FACT (launch date, spread); REASONABLE INFERENCE ("one of the
   longest-running" is a comparative characterization repeated across sources,
   not an official ranking)
   Used in AR: yes (beats 046-048) | Used in EN: yes (beats 046-048)

5. 1978 U.S. Senate hearings
   Claim: The U.S. Senate held hearings (Senate Subcommittee on Health, chaired
   in coverage by Sen. Edward Kennedy) on infant formula promotion in developing
   countries in May 1978; a company spokesperson (Oswald Ballerin) testified
   the company was not responsible for local water/sanitation conditions.
   Source: Commons Library summary of the boycott/hearings timeline.
   Certainty: FACT (hearings occurred, date, general content)
   Used in AR: yes (beat 049) | Used in EN: yes (beat 049)

6. WHO/UNICEF 1979 joint meeting
   Claim: WHO and UNICEF convened an international meeting on infant and young
   child feeding in 1979, initiating the process that led to the Code.
   Source: WHO/UNICEF historical record; referenced in WHO's "International Code
   of Marketing of Breast-Milk Substitutes" (1981) preface materials.
   Certainty: FACT
   Used in AR: yes (beat 051) | Used in EN: yes (beat 051)

7. International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes — adoption
   Claim: Adopted by the World Health Assembly on 21 May 1981 by a vote of
   118 in favour to 1 against; the United States cast the sole negative vote.
   Source: WHO, "International code of marketing of breast-milk substitutes"
   (iris.who.int/handle/10665/40382); Washington Post, "U.S., Almost Alone,
   Casts Ballot Against Code on Baby Formula" (21 May 1981); UPI Archives,
   21 May 1981.
   Certainty: FACT
   Used in AR: yes (beats 053-054) | Used in EN: yes (beats 053-054)
   Note: one UPI-adjacent source states the U.S. was joined by no other "no"
   vote but that Argentina abstained in some accounts; core fact (118-1, U.S.
   sole "no") is corroborated by WHO's own record and contemporaneous press
   and is what the narration states. Abstention count is NOT asserted in either
   script (flagged DISPUTED/OMITTED below).

8. Code provisions
   Claim: The Code bans advertising of covered products to the public, free
   samples to mothers, promotion within health care facilities (including free
   supplies), and gifts/samples to health workers; requires labels to state the
   superiority of breastfeeding and warn of risks of improper preparation.
   Source: WHO, International Code of Marketing of Breast-Milk Substitutes
   (1981), Articles 5, 6, 7, 9; summarized by UNICEF Baby Friendly Initiative
   and IBFAN "International Code" pages.
   Certainty: FACT
   Used in AR: yes (beat 055) | Used in EN: yes (beat 055)

9. The Code does not ban formula
   Claim: The Code explicitly recognizes that breast-milk substitutes may be
   necessary and calls for their proper use when needed; it targets marketing
   practices, not the product's existence.
   Source: WHO Code preamble and Article 1 (statement of aim).
   Certainty: FACT
   Used in AR: yes (beat 056) | Used in EN: yes (beat 056)

10. Continuing violations post-1981
    Claim: Subsequent World Health Assembly resolutions have extended the
    Code's scope over the decades; WHO/UNICEF continue to document ongoing
    violations of the Code worldwide.
    Source: WHO/UNICEF, "Global Breastfeeding Collective — International Code
    of Marketing and Subsequent Resolutions"; WHO/UNICEF/IBFAN periodic
    "Marketing of breast-milk substitutes: national implementation" reports.
    Certainty: FACT (resolutions and ongoing monitoring reports exist);
    REASONABLE INFERENCE (used narratively as "still documented today" without
    citing a specific year's report to avoid overclaiming currency)
    Used in AR: yes (beat 056) | Used in EN: yes (beat 056)

11. New Internationalist 1973 early coverage
    Claim: Some of the earliest public scrutiny of formula marketing practices
    appeared a year before "The Baby Killer," in 1973, in the British magazine
    New Internationalist.
    Source: Referenced widely in historical retrospectives of the controversy
    as an early journalistic antecedent to War on Want's 1974 report.
    Certainty: FACT (widely cited in secondary histories of the controversy;
    not independently re-verified against the original 1973 magazine issue in
    this research pass)
    Used in AR: yes (beat 019) | Used in EN: yes (beat 019)

======================================================================
PHYSIOLOGY / PUBLIC HEALTH
======================================================================

12. Supply-and-demand lactation physiology
    Claim: Suckling stimulates prolactin release, which drives ongoing milk
    production; more frequent/effective suckling sustains supply, while
    replacing breastfeeds with bottles reduces stimulation and can lower
    supply over time. This is a gradual process, not immediate loss after a
    single bottle.
    Source: WHO/UNICEF "Infant and Young Child Feeding" (NCBI Bookshelf
    NBK148970), "The physiological basis of breastfeeding"; UNICEF Europe and
    Central Asia, "Breastfeeding: The science of milk production."
    Certainty: PEER-REVIEWED EVIDENCE
    Used in AR: yes (beats 012, 021-026) | Used in EN: yes (beats 012, 021-026)

13. Unsafe water and formula preparation risk
    Claim: Formula requires clean water, clean equipment, and correct
    measurement every time; where water is unsafe and sanitation poor, formula
    preparation becomes a vector for bacterial contamination and diarrheal
    disease, a leading cause of infant morbidity/mortality in such settings.
    Source: Journal of the American Dietetic Association, "Water and Food
    Safety in the Developing World" (2007); CDC Emerging Infectious Diseases,
    "Saving Lives through Global Safe Water" (2005); WHO/UNICEF data that ~88%
    of diarrheal deaths are attributable to unsafe water/sanitation/hygiene.
    Certainty: PEER-REVIEWED EVIDENCE
    Used in AR: yes (beats 032-037) | Used in EN: yes (beats 032-037)

14. Over-dilution of formula due to cost
    Claim: Some families facing financial strain stretched formula supplies by
    using less powder per bottle than instructed, resulting in under-nutrition
    despite an apparently "fed" infant.
    Source: Contemporary 1970s reports on formula marketing in low-income
    settings (referenced across War on Want and subsequent public-health
    literature); general malnutrition-risk mechanism corroborated by nutrition
    science.
    Certainty: PRIMARY-SOURCE ALLEGATION (specific over-dilution behavior,
    reported but not universally quantified) / PEER-REVIEWED EVIDENCE (that
    diluted formula under-delivers calories/nutrients)
    Used in AR: yes (beats 029-031) | Used in EN: yes (beats 029-031)

15. Mortality research linking formula marketing and water access
    Claim: Economic/public-health research has found that mortality risk
    associated with formula-market entry rose significantly in areas with
    unclean water, but not in areas with clean water access — indicating
    environment, not the product per se, drove the added risk.
    Source: NBER Working Paper 24452, "Mortality from Nestlé's Marketing of
    Infant Formula in Low- and Middle-Income Countries."
    Certainty: PEER-REVIEWED EVIDENCE (NBER working paper; economics research,
    not a randomized clinical study — flagged as such)
    Used in AR: yes (beat 037, general framing) | Used in EN: yes (beat 037)

COMPANY POSITION
======================================================================

16. Company's rebuttal
    Claim: The company disputed responsibility for outcomes tied to water and
    sanitation conditions it said it did not control, and stated that reforms
    to its marketing practices were already under way at the time of the
    controversy.
    Source: Company public statements reported contemporaneously (via Senate
    hearing testimony and press coverage of the Swiss libel case); company's own
    modern historical framing at nestle.com "Marketing infant nutrition:
    getting it right" (retrospective company position, read for balance, not
    treated as neutral fact).
    Certainty: COMPANY POSITION
    Used in AR: yes (beat 045) | Used in EN: yes (beat 045)

======================================================================
DISPUTED / OMITTED CLAIMS
======================================================================

- "Nestlé deliberately intended for babies to die / mothers' milk to dry up."
  OMITTED. This is an inference about intent that cannot be verified from any
  source consulted. The scripts describe only the verified business mechanism
  (free sample → reduced breastfeeding → possible supply decline → recurring
  purchase) and documented marketing tactics, never claimed intent.

- Exact number of countries reached by the INFACT boycott.
  One secondary source states "65 countries"; this figure was not
  independently corroborated against a primary tally, so the scripts use the
  softer "dozens of countries" / "عشرات الدول" rather than a specific number.

- Exact wording of the 1976 Swiss judge's remarks.
  Widely paraphrased identically across secondary sources ("if the company
  did not want to be accused of causing death and illness through its sales
  practices, it should change how it does business") but the original German
  court-record wording was not independently obtained. Treated as
  PRIMARY-SOURCE ALLEGATION rather than a verbatim quotation; not put in
  quotation marks in the narration.

- Whether any country besides the U.S. abstained or voted against the 1981
  WHA Code vote. WHO's own summary and contemporaneous press agree on "118 to
  1," with the U.S. as the sole dissenting vote; the narration follows this
  consensus figure and does not assert an abstention count.

- The real company's name and brand identity.
  Per task instructions (Rule 6), never named or visually depicted in the
  final video. Referred to only as "a Swiss food giant" / "one of the world's
  biggest food companies" / "شركة سويسرية عملاقة." Internal research (this
  file, arabic_pronunciation_risk.txt) references the real company by name
  once for verification purposes only, consistent with the brief's instruction
  to research internally without naming it publicly.
