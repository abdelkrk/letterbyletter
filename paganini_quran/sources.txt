SOURCES — Paganini Qur'an Documentary
Compiled from web research (WebSearch snippets; direct WebFetch to several primary domains was blocked by network egress policy in this environment — noted where relevant). Claims are cross-checked across multiple independent secondary sources summarizing the primary scholarship below.

============================================================
1. CLAIM: Paganino Paganini (c.1450–1538) was a Venetian printer; also published Luca Pacioli's mathematical works (Summa de arithmetica, De divina proportione).
SOURCE: Wikipedia, "Paganino Paganini" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganino_Paganini)
CERTAINTY: Documented fact (standard bibliographic history).

2. CLAIM: Paganino and his son Alessandro Paganini printed the first known complete edition of the Qur'an in Arabic using movable type, in Venice, with the print dated to the period between August 1537 and August 1538.
SOURCES: History of Information, "Paganino & Alessandro Paganini Issue the First Printed Edition of the Qur'an in Arabic, of Which One Copy Survived" (historyofinformation.com); Wikipedia "Paganino Paganini"; Institute for Advanced Study, R. Tottoli, "Editing the Qur'an in Sixteenth- and Seventeenth-Century Europe" (ias.edu/ideas/2017/tottoli-quran); Madain Project, "Paganini Quran"
CERTAINTY: Documented fact — dating is based on the colophon/bibliographic record cited across sources.

3. CLAIM: The edition was probably produced for export/sale to the Ottoman Empire, with which Venice had extensive commercial ties.
SOURCES: History of Information; Wikipedia "Paganino Paganini"; Madain Project
CERTAINTY: Scholarly inference (probable intended market), explicitly framed as "probably" / "likely" in sources — not a certainty.

4. CLAIM: Arabic connected/cursive script posed a fundamentally different technical challenge for European movable-type printing than the Latin alphabet (positional letterforms, ligatures).
SOURCES: American Printing History Association, "Neither Good, Fast, Nor Cheap: Challenges of Early Arabic Letterpress Printing" (printinghistory.org); Yale University Library, "Printing History in the Arabic-Speaking World" exhibition notes; general history-of-printing scholarship on early Arabic type-cutting difficulty (Robert Granjon, Erpenius generation).
CERTAINTY: Documented fact (well-established in history-of-printing scholarship).

5. CLAIM: The surviving copy contains a very large number of textual/typographic errors — described by scholars as having scarcely a single page printed without fault.
SOURCES: History of Information (summarizing Angela Nuovo's findings); multiple secondary summaries repeating this characterization.
CERTAINTY: Documented fact, based on direct examination of the surviving 1987-discovered copy by specialists in Arabic.

6. CLAIM: The entire print run was reported/believed lost for centuries; the edition passed into scholarly memory as a book known through references but not seen.
SOURCES: History of Information; Wikipedia "Paganino Paganini"; Madain Project
CERTAINTY: Documented fact — corroborated by the existence of Giovanni Bernardo de Rossi's 18th-century dissertation about the edition (see #7), written without access to a surviving physical copy.

7. CLAIM: In the 18th century, Italian orientalist Giovanni Bernardo de Rossi wrote a dissertation, "De corano arabico Venetiis Paganini typis impresso," discussing the Paganini Qur'an based on historical references, without having examined a surviving copy.
SOURCES: Antiquariat INLIBRIS listing for De Rossi's dissertation (inlibris.com); cross-referenced via multiple bibliographic/antiquarian listings.
CERTAINTY: Documented fact (bibliographic record of the dissertation's existence and subject).

8. CLAIM: A popular legend held that the Pope ordered the entire print run burned; this explanation is NOT supported by a documented primary source and is treated by historians as a later, unconfirmed legend among several competing (and sometimes far-fetched) explanations for the edition's disappearance.
SOURCES: WebSearch synthesis of History of Information and related secondary sources explicitly describing reasons for the loss as ranging "from suppression by the Pope to the ridiculous," and characterizing the papal-burning story as unconfirmed/legendary rather than documented.
CERTAINTY: Explicitly flagged in the script as LEGEND, not fact — the narration states this directly and does not assert the burning as historical.

9. CLAIM: In 1987, Italian scholar Angela Nuovo, while researching Paganini's typography/printing output, discovered a surviving copy of the Qur'an in the library of the Franciscan Friars (Frati Minori) of San Michele in Isola, Venice.
SOURCES: Madain Project, "Paganini Quran"; History of Information; Wikipedia "San Michele in Isola"
CERTAINTY: Documented fact.

10. CLAIM: Angela Nuovo published her findings as "Il Corano arabo ritrovato" in La Bibliofilia, 1987, pp. 237–271; an English translation, "A Lost Arabic Koran Rediscovered," appeared in The Library, 1990.
SOURCES: WebSearch aggregation of bibliographic/antiquarian records (abebooks.fr listings referencing De Rossi/Nuovo scholarship; scholarly citation patterns cross-referenced across multiple library-history secondary sources).
CERTAINTY: Documented fact (standard citation, corroborated across multiple secondary mentions); could not independently verify exact page range via a primary database due to network access restrictions in this environment — reported with appropriate confidence as a widely-repeated bibliographic citation.

11. CLAIM: The rediscovered copy bears an ownership note of Teseo Ambrogio degli Albonesi, an orientalist scholar who died soon after 1540 and who referenced this Qur'an in his own 1539 published work, "Introductio in Chaldaicam linguam, Syriacam atque Armenicam et decem alias linguas."
SOURCES: Madain Project, "Paganini Quran"
CERTAINTY: Documented fact (provenance mark on the surviving physical copy, and matching a contemporary printed reference by Albonesi).

12. CLAIM: The surviving copy also bears the stamp of Arcangelo Mancasula, Vicar of the Holy Office (Inquisition) of Cremona, applied some years after the ownership note.
SOURCES: Madain Project, "Paganini Quran"
CERTAINTY: Documented fact (physical provenance mark); the narration does NOT claim to know why the book was in Inquisition hands — that remains unstated in available sources, and the script frames it as an open question.

13. CLAIM: The rediscovery was internationally significant for the history of printing, Arabic typography, and Venetian–Ottoman/European–Islamic cultural and commercial relations; the discovery was reported and translated into multiple languages including English and Arabic.
SOURCES: Multiple secondary summaries (WebSearch aggregation); Institute for Advanced Study (Tottoli) discussing the broader significance of Qur'an editing/printing history in this period.
CERTAINTY: Documented / broadly corroborated scholarly assessment.

14. CLAIM: The surviving copy is preserved today in the library of the Franciscan Friars of San Michele in Isola, Venice.
SOURCES: Madain Project, "Paganini Quran"
CERTAINTY: Documented fact as of the sources consulted.

15. CLAIM: The fate of the remainder of the print run remains genuinely unknown; scholars have not established with certainty why or how the edition disappeared beyond the one surviving copy.
SOURCES: Synthesis of all above sources — none provide a confirmed, documented, single explanation; several explicitly frame the cause as uncertain/debated.
CERTAINTY: This is treated in the narration as the central acknowledged UNKNOWN, per editorial instruction — no false certainty is asserted.
